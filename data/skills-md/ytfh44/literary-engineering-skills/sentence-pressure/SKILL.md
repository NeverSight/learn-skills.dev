# Sentence Pressure

## Use when

The sentence lengths may look varied while the information arrives at one
dead speed, or a long sentence keeps adding clauses without one continuous
path — or the prose moves badly in the mouth and ear even though the
information timing is fine.

## Do not use when

Do not prescribe "alternate long and short sentences." Length is an output,
never a rule. Do not treat rhythm as one thing: semantic pressure and
kinetic/prosodic pressure are different dimensions and can disagree.

## Core test

**Rhythm is the timing of information and the motion of the sentence —
not the histogram of sentence lengths.**

Two tracks, two questions:

- **Semantic pressure:** when does the reader receive, withhold, revise, or
  resolve information?
- **Kinetic/prosodic pressure:** how does the sentence move in the mouth,
  ear, and working memory — stress beats, breath units, syntactic momentum,
  parallelism, ritual repetition?

## Track A — semantic pressure

1. Mark each sentence's new propositions.
2. Mark uncertainty introduced and uncertainty released.
3. Identify the sentence's load-bearing path: action, attention, argument,
   inventory, or interruption.
4. Split when one sentence carries unrelated paths.
5. Join when several short sentences fracture one continuous event.
6. Keep repetition only if each recurrence changes pressure, expectation, or
   scale.
7. Read punctuation as scheduling: where is the reader forced to wait?

**Pressure operations:**

- **accumulate** — each clause adds a constraint before release;
- **delay** — postpone the decisive noun/verb;
- **cut** — terminate a path early;
- **echo** — repeat with changed consequence;
- **release** — resolve the question a prior clause opened;
- **reset** — clear one scene unit before the next.

## Track B — kinetic/prosodic pressure

1. Read the sentence aloud (or subvocally): where do the stress beats land?
2. Mark breath units — the chunks a speaker can produce without gasping.
3. Check syntactic momentum: does the clause chain run forward, stall, or
   loop?
4. Test parallelism: are repeated structures carrying weight, or just
   marching?
5. Distinguish ritual repetition (incantatory, structural) from filler
   repetition (dead weight).
6. Count prosodic weight: long open vowels, heavy consonant clusters, and
   monosyllabic strings change how a sentence feels to say.

The two tracks can disagree completely: a paragraph of long, flowing,
phonetically beautiful sentences can deliver information at one dead speed
(high kinetic, low semantic); a string of choppy monosyllables can sustain
real suspense by closing escape routes (low kinetic, high semantic).

## Minimal pair

> 他开门。走进去。看见桌上的杯子。停住了。
>
> 他开门走进去，直到看见桌上的杯子才停住。

B is not better because it is longer. One continuous motion now carries a
delayed stopping condition. If each step needs separate hesitation, A may
be right.

Kinetic pair:

> 他沿着走廊走下去，走廊的灯一盏一盏地亮起来，又在他身后一盏一盏地
> 灭掉。
>
> 灯亮，灯灭，他走。

If the scene's information is the measured alternation, the second's
monosyllabic beats are the event. If the scene needs the walk's continuity,
the first carries it. Neither is "more rhythmic" by rule; the question is
which track the scene is on.

## Counterexamples

- A sequence of short sentences can sustain pressure when each closes one
  escape route.
- A long inventory sentence can be right when simultaneity and accumulation
  are the point, even without a dramatic release.
- A deliberately flat, breathless narrator may strip both tracks as a voice
  choice; the flatness is the voice, not a defect.
- Ritual or liturgical repetition may be pure kinetic weight with zero
  information change per iteration; audit it as ritual, not as redundancy.

## Rule strength

**Discourse tests.** No length-mixing rules, no average-sentence-length
targets, no "vary your sentence structure" advice. The reliable operations
are per-track diagnosis (information path vs. phonetic motion), boundary
changes (split/join), and one-variable interventions that move a single
track.

## Return shape

Return **track -> information path or phonetic motion -> pressure failure ->
boundary change -> predicted reading change**. Do not justify a revision
with "more varied sentence lengths" or "better rhythm" — name the track and
the change it makes.

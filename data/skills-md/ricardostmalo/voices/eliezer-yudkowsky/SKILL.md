# Eliezer Yudkowsky

A writer who cares more about being right than about being liked, and who thinks most writing is imprecise in ways that matter. Dense, exact, and structured around the difference between what is true and what is comfortable.

## Structure

- Often opens with a concrete scenario or a mistake someone made, then generalizes.
- Builds concepts step by step, naming each one, sometimes coining a term for it.
- Long paragraphs with nested qualifications. Parentheses inside parentheses are normal.
- Frequently anticipates the misreading and heads it off: "This does not mean X."
- Ends on the sharpest form of the point, sometimes a single sentence.

## Sentence habits

- Precise to the point of pedantry, on purpose. "Probably" and "almost certainly" are different words and used differently.
- Italics for the word that carries the distinction.
- Parenthetical asides that contain real content, not jokes.
- Second person to address the reader's likely reasoning error directly.
- Long sentences that hold several conditions at once. Short sentences for verdicts.
- Occasional dry humor, deadpan, embedded in a serious sentence.

## Moves he makes

- Names the exact cognitive error: "This is the conjunction fallacy," and explains it.
- Asks what you would expect to observe if the belief were false.
- Distinguishes the map from the territory, the model from the thing.
- Refuses to soften a conclusion because it is unpleasant.
- Uses thought experiments with explicit setup and explicit conclusion.

## Never

- No vagueness. If a term could mean two things, define which.
- No appeals to authority without the argument.
- No "both sides have a point" when one side is wrong.
- No motivational close.
- No hedging to seem humble; hedge only to be accurate.

## Example

Before: "It's important to consider multiple perspectives when evaluating AI risk."

After: "If you find yourself weighing 'perspectives' on whether a system will do what you want, stop and ask what specific observation would tell you it won't. If no observation would, you are not weighing evidence; you are choosing a mood."

## Check

Read each sentence and ask: could a hostile, intelligent reader misinterpret this? If yes, add the qualifier. If a paragraph makes the reader feel comfortable without teaching them anything, cut it.

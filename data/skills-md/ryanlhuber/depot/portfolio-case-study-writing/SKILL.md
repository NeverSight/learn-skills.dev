# Portfolio Case Study Writing

## Purpose
Gather a designer's project facts through targeted questions and turn them into an honest, decision-focused case study suitable for a portfolio.

## When to use
- Use when: a designer wants a case study written from their own project experience.
- Do not use when: the project facts are unavailable to the designer; do not write a case study from invention.

## Inputs
- Required: the designer's answers about the project (see Process step 2), or existing notes, artifacts, and write-ups from which answers can be extracted.
- Optional: the intended audience (for example hiring managers or clients), length and format constraints, available visuals and artifacts, confidentiality or NDA terms, links to supporting evidence.
- Assumptions: any detail not stated by the designer is treated as unknown, not filled in.

## Process
1. Extract what the inputs already answer, and mark the remaining gaps.
2. Ask the designer only for the missing items, in short groups:
   - Project: what it was, for whom, when, and its scale and constraints.
   - Role: what they personally did, what the team did, and who else contributed.
   - Problem: who was affected, what was wrong, and how it was known.
   - Process: the key steps taken and why.
   - Decisions: the main choices, the alternatives considered, and the reasons for each.
   - Outcome: what changed, with evidence (measurements, feedback, observed behavior) and its limits.
   - Reflection: what did not work, what they would change, and what they learned.
   - Confidentiality: what may and may not be shown or named.
3. Resolve unanswered or unclear items by asking again or marking them as gaps; do not infer them.
4. Choose a narrative that leads with the problem and the designer's decisions: context, problem, role, process, key decisions, outcome, reflection.
5. Write each section from the designer's answers, in the first person unless told otherwise, with concrete specifics and no filler.
6. Label each outcome claim by basis: measured result, qualitative feedback, or the designer's judgment.
7. For each section, suggest the visual or artifact that best evidences it (for example a before and after, a diagram of the flow, a test finding), and note anything that must be redacted.
8. List remaining gaps and the evidence that would close each.

## Rules and heuristics
- Required: never invent metrics, quotes, outcomes, titles, tools, or timelines. Unknown becomes a labeled gap.
- Required: distinguish individual contribution from team contribution; do not claim team work as solo work.
- Required: respect stated confidentiality limits; generalize or omit protected detail and say what was changed.
- Required: do not present a correlation or a stakeholder opinion as a proven result of the design.
- Recommended: explain the reasoning behind decisions, including rejected options, rather than listing deliverables.
- Recommended: include a candid reflection; it signals judgment.
- Recommended: keep it scannable, with one main idea per section.

## Checks
- Every factual claim traces to the designer's answers or supplied material.
- Role and team contributions are separated.
- Every outcome claim carries its basis and limits.
- No protected information appears beyond what the designer approved.
- Gaps are listed, not hidden by confident phrasing.

## Output
Return the draft case study with section headings, a visual and artifact checklist per section, a list of redactions or generalizations applied, and remaining gaps with the evidence needed to close each.

## Completion criteria
- The draft covers context, problem, role, process, decisions, outcome, and reflection, or states why a section is missing.
- All claims are sourced to the designer or labeled as gaps.
- Confidentiality limits are applied and reported.

# Storytelling Hooks: The Six Psychology Levers

- Short-form content lives or dies in the first 2 seconds.
- A strong hook stacks 2–3 levers in one line; a full script runs them in order.
- **A hook has two jobs: stop the scroll AND filter for the right viewer.** A hook that grabs everyone attracts the wrong audience — they leave at second 3, retention tanks, and the algorithm buries the video. Qualifying words ("freelancers", "if you run ads") cost a little reach and buy retention plus the right follows.
- This is the **core skill** of the storytelling suite. Other skills (long-form-youtube, titles-and-thumbnails, carousel-narratives, email-hooks, wireframe-writer, wireframe-reviewer) recap these levers and link back here.

## When to Use

- Writing a reel / Short / TikTok script or opening line.
- Any niche or business: education, SaaS, fitness, finance, faith, food, B2B, personal brand.
- The moment must stop the scroll before the brain decides to move on.
- When a draft feels flat and you need to diagnose which lever is missing.

## The Six Levers (Quick Reference)

| # | Lever | One-line job |
|---|-------|--------------|
| 1 | Curiosity gap | Open a loop, don't close it |
| 2 | Emotional mirror | Target the feeling, not the fact |
| 3 | Conflict engine | Goal + obstacle = tension |
| 4 | Relatability | Broadest shared feeling → "this is me" |
| 5 | Pattern + surprise | Set expectation, then break it |
| 6 | Three-act structure | Setup → problem → resolution |

---

### 1. Curiosity Gap
- **What:** Open a question, don't answer it. The brain hates an unfinished loop.
- **Why:** An incomplete pattern creates tension; attention holds at the gap until it closes.
- **Move:** Reference something specific but unnamed — "this mistake", "this one setting", "the one habit". End on a cliff: "...and that's when everything changed."
- **Stronger:** Add a stake (reward gained or problem removed) → two gaps: "what is it?" + "what happens?"
- **Example:** "Most people lose money on this app without ever noticing the one toggle that's draining it."

### 2. Emotional Mirror
- **What:** Target a feeling, not just a fact.
- **Why:** Naming a feeling the viewer already carries makes them re-feel it in the moment — the content becomes about *them*.
- **Move:** Swap dry information for the emotion underneath it.
- **Example:** Fact → "Here's a tip for staying productive." Mirror → "If your to-do list makes you feel behind before the day even starts, this is for you."

### 3. Conflict Engine
- **What:** A goal plus an obstacle. No obstacle = no tension.
- **Why:** The brain invests in unresolved tension: "will it work out or not?"
- **Move:** Name what's wanted, then what blocks it (a common mistake, a hidden trap, a force).
- **False obstacles:** The most gripping conflicts are often self-made — a fear or excuse the person creates. Applies to characters and to the viewer being addressed.
- **Example:** "You're doing everything right to grow — and one invisible habit is quietly capping your results."

### 4. Relatability
- **What:** Pick a feeling or situation your target audience shares. Universal *within the audience you want* — not universal to everyone.
- **Why:** People connect to what they see themselves in. But maximum breadth attracts viewers who won't stay or follow; relatability serves qualification, not raw reach.
- **Move:** Choose the broadest shared experience *inside your niche*, and keep a qualifying signal in the line.
- **Trade-off:** Vivid detail adds texture but can shrink reach. "Waking at 4am in astronaut training" relates to no one; "waking up tired" relates to everyone — but "waking up tired" attracts everyone and converts no one. "Waking up tired because a client's revisions kept you up" relates to exactly the freelancers you want.
- **Example:** "Ever sent a message, then refreshed your phone every 10 seconds waiting for the reply?"

### 5. Pattern + Surprise
- **What:** Set an expectation, then break it.
- **Why:** The brain predicts what's next; a prediction broken in an interesting way grabs attention and makes the moment stick.
- **Move:** "Everything looks fine... but...". The word "but" is the switch. Surprise needs a setup first.
- **Example:** "She followed every rule in the productivity book. But the one thing that finally worked wasn't in it."

### 6. Three-Act Structure
- **What:** The skeleton of every story.
  1. **Setup** — normal world, everything ordinary.
  2. **Problem** — tension, mistake, or danger enters.
  3. **Resolution** — the outcome, carrying a lesson or feeling.
- **Why:** Gives the brain a clear arc (stability → disruption → release) that feels complete.
- **Example:** ordinary launch day → sales flatline and panic → the single change that turned it around.

## The Three Hook Layers (Short-Form Video)

A Reel/Short/TikTok hook is not one line — it's three elements landing at 0:00, and most feeds autoplay **muted**:

| Layer | What it is | Rule |
|-------|-----------|------|
| Visual first frame | What's on screen at 0:00 | Must intrigue with sound off — motion, an odd object, a mid-action moment |
| Spoken line | The first sentence | Where the levers live (this skill) |
| Text overlay | On-screen text | Complements the spoken line, never duplicates it — the other half of the gap |

- Overlay + spoken line pair like a title + thumbnail: one names the gap, the other shows the stakes/emotion.
- Always deliver all three layers when writing a video hook, not just the spoken line.

## Generation Workflow

When asked to write a hook or script:

1. **Intake.** Confirm before writing: niche, *exact* audience (who should follow, not just who might watch), platform, goal (follows / saves / sales / views), and tone. If key facts are missing, ask up to 2 questions when the user can respond; otherwise state your assumptions explicitly at the top and proceed.
2. **Variants.** Read **patterns.md** (same folder) first — fill-in structures per lever, first-frame concepts, overlay pairings — then write 5+ hook options, each stacking 2–3 levers, levers labeled. Adapt the patterns to the niche; never reuse a worked example verbatim, even when it matches the topic.
3. **Select.** Score against the Pre-Publish Checklist; recommend one and say why (which gaps it opens, who it qualifies).
4. **Script.** Expand the winner using the lever order below, specifying all three hook layers for the opening.

## Bad → Good Rewrite

- ❌ "Here are 3 budgeting tips for freelancers." — a topic label: no gap, no feeling, no tension. Nothing is at stake and nothing is unresolved.
- ✅ "You landed a $3,000 client last month — so why is your bank account still empty?" — relatability + emotional mirror (the fear behind the balance) + curiosity gap (why?) + a concrete number, and "client" qualifies for freelancers.
- The delta: the fact was replaced by the *feeling underneath the fact*, and a closed statement became an open question with stakes.

## Building a Full Hook or Script

- Most short scripts are 3–6 lines (~20–45 seconds spoken; ~2.5 words/second is a workable pace check). Run the levers in order:
1. **Open (1 + 4, often + 2):** curiosity gap wrapped in a relatable feeling. The scroll-stopper. Must work in 2 seconds.
2. **Build tension (3):** introduce the obstacle or common mistake; raise stakes.
3. **Twist if possible (5):** break the expectation you set. Optional but powerful.
4. **Pay off (6 resolution):** resolve with the lesson or emotional point.
5. **Close with a bookend** (below).

- **Worked example (stacked):**
  - "Ever feel like you're working harder than everyone but falling behind? *(relatability + mirror)*
  - Most people blame their schedule — the real leak is one habit they repeat every morning. *(conflict + curiosity gap)*
  - Fix that one thing and the whole day finally clicks." *(resolution + mirror)*

## The Bookend Technique & CTA

- Repeat a key phrase or number from the hook in the final line.
- Start and end touching the same phrase = the brain registers "completion" → satisfying, and pushes the viewer to comment/share.
- **One CTA, matched to the intake goal:** follows → "I post [X] for [audience] every week"; saves → "save this for your next [moment]"; comments → ask one specific question. Fold the CTA into the bookend line rather than tacking it on after.
- Overlays aren't only for 0:00 — flash the key phrase of each beat (twist, payoff) as on-screen text to serve muted viewers throughout.
- **Tip:** Concrete numbers and time gaps ("3 times", "in 7 days", "for 5 years") sharpen a hook. Reach for a specific number whenever facts allow.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Fix |
|---------|-----|
| Hook answers its own question | Keep the gap open until the payoff line |
| Hook chases everyone ("stop scrolling!") | Add a qualifying word — the *right* viewer beats the most viewers |
| Only the spoken line written | Specify visual first frame + text overlay too |
| Overlay repeats the spoken line | Overlay carries the other half of the gap |
| Two ideas in one hook | One gap per hook; save the second for another post |
| Gap the video never pays off | Clickbait kills retention and trust — close every loop you open |
| Vague where a number exists | "3 steps", "$3,000", "in 7 days" beat "a few", "a lot", "quickly" |

## Pre-Publish Checklist

- [ ] Does line 1 open a curiosity gap, working in under 2 seconds?
- [ ] Is a real feeling targeted, not a dry fact?
- [ ] Is there a goal + obstacle creating tension?
- [ ] Is the core feeling universal *within the target audience* — and does a qualifying signal filter for the right viewer?
- [ ] Are all three hook layers specified (first frame, spoken line, overlay)?
- [ ] Is there at least one moment that breaks expectation?
- [ ] Does it move through setup → problem → resolution?
- [ ] Does the final line bookend back to the opening?
- If a line is flat, ask which lever is missing and add it.

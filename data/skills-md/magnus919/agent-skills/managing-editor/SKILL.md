# Managing Editor

Run a dependable editorial operation from request to published record. A managing editor makes the work legible, keeps it moving, protects the publication's standards, and connects people who own different decisions. The title does **not** imply universal authority over editorial direction or final publication: establish the local decision rights first.

## Start with the operating agreement

Before assigning work, establish:

- **Product and audience:** what is being made, for whom, where it will appear, and what success means.
- **Editorial owner:** who controls scope, voice, acceptance, and final sign-off.
- **Managing editor's remit:** which stages, resources, schedules, contributors, and quality checks you own.
- **Specialist approvals:** who must clear factual, scientific, legal, privacy, rights, safety, accessibility, brand, or executive concerns.
- **Constraints:** publication date and time zone, length/format, budget and capacity, review windows, embargoes, confidentiality, and platform limits.
- **Authority and escalation:** who can change scope or date, accept residual risk, resolve disputes, and authorize correction or withdrawal.

Write down ambiguous boundaries. A RACI or simple `does / decides / approves / advises` table is useful; do not let it imply approval by a person who has not agreed to own it. Keep one named accountable human for editorial sign-off.

## End-to-end workflow

Adapt the stages to the publication. Some work, especially peer review or legal review, has its own mandatory sequence.

1. **Intake and triage.** Capture the request, intended audience, value, format, deadline, source material, dependencies, sensitivity, and requester. Check fit and feasibility. Accept, defer, decline, or request missing information with a reason.
2. **Plan the portfolio.** Put accepted work on an editorial calendar or queue with one accountable owner, one next action, stage dates, dependency links, and a status. Reserve capacity for urgent work and corrections. Do not treat every request as a firm commitment.
3. **Commission and brief.** Give the contributor a clear assignment: purpose, audience, format, angle or question, length, deliverables, source/evidence needs, house style, rights/accessibility requirements, review path, dates, and acceptance criteria. State what is out of scope.
4. **Coordinate research and drafting.** Keep source material and draft versions traceable. Agree check-ins at risk points, not for every keystroke. Raise scope, access, source, or deadline problems early.
5. **Edit in deliberate passes.** Settle purpose, structure, and argument before sentence polish. Separate developmental/substantive edits, fact and source verification, copyediting, and proof review when the stakes or workflow justify distinct passes. Mark queries clearly; preserve the author's meaning and voice unless the assignment calls for rewriting.
6. **Resolve reviews and approvals.** Consolidate comments, resolve conflicts against the brief and governing standard, identify who decides, and record unresolved points. A reviewer recommendation is not automatically an editorial decision. Protect blind review and confidential material where applicable.
7. **Release to production.** Confirm the approved text/version, title/deck/captions, sources and links, permissions and releases, figures/assets and alt text, metadata, author details, layout or platform requirements, and any required approvals. Name who will proof the rendered output.
8. **Publish and maintain the record.** Check the live or printed result against the approved master. Monitor meaningful early feedback. Log corrections, updates, withdrawals, and their rationale; keep a clear version-of-record trail where the publication requires one.
9. **Review the operation.** Compare expected and actual dates, rework, missed handoffs, quality issues, and workload. Find the process cause before blaming an individual. Change one useful part of the system and check whether it helped.

Use stage gates when a missed check could create harm, a rights problem, an irreversible release, or expensive downstream rework. For routine work, use a lightweight queue and explicit next action. Read [workflow and quality methods](references/workflow-and-quality.md) for selection and gate design.

## Prioritize and solve problems

When work is overloaded or blocked:

1. Restate the outcome, audience, hard date, and decision owner.
2. List work in progress, remaining effort, review waits, dependencies, and the blocking decision. Look for waiting, unclear ownership, late scope change, rework, or an unavailable specialist.
3. Protect non-negotiable standards and approvals. Separate the true deadline from a preferred date.
4. Offer the decision owner explicit options: reduce scope, move the date, add capacity, change format, or stop. Show the cost and risk of each. Do not quietly trade accuracy, consent, accessibility, or required review for speed.
5. Record the decision, owner, time, and next checkpoint. Tell affected people directly.

Escalate suspected factual error, fabrication, plagiarism, undisclosed conflict, privacy breach, legal threat, safety issue, research misconduct, rights gap, or pressure to alter editorial judgment through the publication's designated route. Preserve evidence and restrict access while the responsible specialist or editor assesses it. Do not make legal, medical, scientific-validity, or disciplinary findings outside your remit.

## Delegate work with explicit contracts

Delegation transfers a task, not accountability for the publication. Give every human contributor or subagent a bounded task packet. Assign tasks in parallel only when their inputs and outputs are independent; use explicit handoffs for dependent work.

For each delegated task, state:

- one owner and one deliverable;
- purpose, reader, context, and source files/links;
- allowed methods and prohibited actions, including any external contact or publication prohibition;
- required evidence, citation format, privacy limits, and treatment of uncertainty;
- acceptance criteria, output location/format, due time with time zone, and escalation trigger;
- the next reviewer or handoff and who retains the decision.

For subagents, share only necessary context. Do not send unpublished/confidential manuscripts, personal data, source identities, embargoed material, credentials, or licensed content to an agent or service unless its use is authorized for that material. Do not ask an agent to contact sources, accept terms, alter shared records, submit, schedule, or publish unless the user and system have authorized that action. Never invent sources, quotations, interviews, approvals, fact checks, peer review, or test results.

Keep research, drafting, critique, and verification distinguishable. A second agent can find counterevidence, test citation-to-claim links, or flag unsupported statements; it cannot turn a claim true merely by agreeing with the drafter. The accountable editor inspects consequential evidence and resolves disagreements. Read [delegation and editorial accountability](references/delegation-and-accountability.md) for a sample staged workflow and safe handoff protocol.

## Communicate so work can move

- Put the decision, owner, due date, and next action near the top of a message.
- Give contributors one source of truth for the current brief, version, status, and comments. Avoid parallel conflicting instructions.
- Make requests specific and respectful: identify the passage or deliverable, explain the reader or standard affected, and ask a bounded question.
- Distinguish required corrections from preferences; explain when the contributor may propose an equivalent solution.
- Share bad news early: what changed, what it affects, options, the decision needed, and by when.
- Close a handoff with what is complete, what is uncertain, open questions, and the recipient's next action.
- Scale the communication cadence to deadline risk and review latency. Keep a concise decision log for scope, schedule, standards exceptions, and approvals.

## When not to use

Do not use this skill as a substitute for domain-specific reporting, scholarly peer review, legal advice, accessibility expertise, copyediting style rules, design, or a platform runbook. Load the relevant specialist skill and keep the managing editor responsible for coordinating the handoff and publication decision within their granted authority.

## Completion

The assignment is complete when the authorized final version is published or handed to its next named owner, required decisions and approvals are recorded, open risks are disclosed, the output matches its approved master, and corrections or follow-up work have an owner. If publication cannot proceed safely or on the agreed schedule, present the decision options to the person with authority and document the outcome.

# Orwell Clear Writing

Make the meaning easy to see without making the writing flat, evasive, or less accurate.

## Workflow

1. Identify the text's audience, purpose, and non-negotiable meaning. Preserve necessary technical or legal precision.
2. Revise the draft against the six rules below. Prefer the smallest change that improves clarity.
3. Read the result as a whole. Restore a rule-breaking construction when it is the clearest, most natural, or least misleading choice.
4. Return the revised text. When useful, add a brief list of consequential edits or unresolved ambiguities; do not explain every minor substitution.

## The six rules

Apply these in order, but treat the sixth as an explicit override:

1. Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech that you are used to seeing in print. Replace stale imagery with a concrete fact or a fresh, necessary image.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active. Name the actor when that improves accountability or understanding.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or jargon if you can think of an everyday English equivalent. Keep terms of art when replacing them would reduce accuracy, then define them briefly for a general audience.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous. Do not sacrifice precision, tone, rhythm, safety, or fairness merely to comply mechanically.

## Editing checks

Use these quick tests while revising:

- Replace abstractions with the person, action, object, amount, date, or decision they conceal.
- Cut throat-clearing, duplicated qualifiers, filler transitions, and nominalizations when the sentence still says the same thing.
- Prefer a verb over a noun built from one: write "decided" rather than "made a decision."
- Change passive voice only when the actor is known and matters. Retain it when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, deliberately protected, or the receiver of the action is the focus.
- Do not conceal responsibility: prefer "We delayed the release" to "The release was delayed" when the author is responsible and naming them is relevant.
- Preserve quoted text, defined terms, names, numbers, and requirements unless asked to change them.
- Flag a vague claim or missing actor instead of inventing one.

## Output modes

Match the request:

- **Rewrite:** Provide only the polished text unless a rationale is requested.
- **Edit with explanation:** Provide the revision, then 3–6 high-impact changes grouped by rule.
- **Draft:** Write directly in plain English, then do one editing pass using the checks above.
- **Light edit:** Keep the author's voice and structure; correct only clear violations that obstruct meaning.

## Examples

- "At this point in time, the implementation of the policy is being undertaken by the team." → "The team is now carrying out the policy."
- "The results were not entirely unexpected." → "We expected these results." (Use only if the writer truly means this.)
- "We will leverage a synergistic framework." → "We will use a shared approach."

Do not treat simplification as a license to change the writer's claim. Ask a concise question or mark an uncertainty when the intended meaning cannot be recovered from the text.
